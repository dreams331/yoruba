## Origins

Oral tradition places the founding of Oyo with Oranmiyan, a prince of Ile-Ife, who is remembered as the first Alaafin (ruler) of Oyo. The early capital, Oyo-Ile (also called Katunga), lay in the savanna north of the forest belt, in what is now Oyo State, Nigeria. Its position mattered: it sat between the forest kingdoms of the south and the Sahel trade routes to the north, which gave it access to both horses and the goods of the coast.

Oyo's early history was not smooth. Tradition recalls a period of defeat by the Nupe in the sixteenth century, after which the court withdrew to Oyo-Igboho. When the capital returned to Oyo-Ile, Oyo rebuilt its army around cavalry, and that change shaped the next two centuries.

## Government and checks on power

The Alaafin was a sacred ruler, but his power was deliberately limited. A council of seven senior chiefs, the Oyo Mesi, advised him and held the right to withdraw their support. Their head, the Bashorun, oversaw the rituals of kingship and could, in extreme cases, tell an Alaafin that the people had rejected him. The Ogboni society represented the cult of the earth and mediated between rulers and the community. Provinces were governed through appointed officials, and the Aare Ona Kakanfo commanded the army in the field and was stationed away from the capital, which reduced the risk of a coup.

This arrangement made Oyo more stable than many contemporary states, but it also produced tension. Strong Bashoruns such as Gaa, in the eighteenth century, were able to dominate the throne, and the contest between king and council later weakened the empire.

## Cavalry, trade and expansion

Horses could not be bred successfully in the forest because of disease carried by the tsetse fly, so Oyo purchased them from the north and kept its army supplied through trade. Cavalry gave Oyo an advantage on open ground, and the empire used it to extend its authority over neighbouring Yoruba kingdoms, over parts of what is now Benin and over areas near the coast. By the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries Oyo controlled one of the largest states in West Africa.

Its wealth came from tribute, from taxes on trade routes and from control of access to the Atlantic coast through ports such as Porto-Novo and Ouidah. Oyo also took part in the Atlantic slave trade, selling captives taken in war, an aspect of its history that historians treat as central to understanding both its prosperity and its later difficulties. At the same time, Oyo's craftspeople produced cloth, leatherwork, ironwork and carved wood, and the court supported poets, drummers and ritual specialists.

## Decline

Several pressures converged from the late eighteenth century. Dahomey, which had long paid tribute to Oyo, ended its dependence in the 1820s. Disputes over succession and the power of the Bashorun weakened the court. In the early nineteenth century Afonja, the Aare Ona Kakanfo based at Ilorin, rebelled and sought support from Muslim scholars and soldiers, and Ilorin soon passed under Fulani leadership and joined the Sokoto Caliphate. Without its northern provinces, Oyo lost much of its cavalry and revenue. Oyo-Ile was abandoned around 1835, and the court moved south to the present town of Oyo.

## Legacy

The collapse of Oyo set off decades of conflict among Yoruba states, including the rise of Ibadan and the long wars of the nineteenth century. Yet Oyo's influence remained. Many Yoruba institutions, including the title of Alaafin, the Ogboni and praise poetry, trace their forms to its court, and the Oyo dialect became the base of the standard Yoruba language. Oyo-Ile survives today as an archaeological site, and the Alaafin of Oyo still holds one of the most senior titles in Yorubaland.

## Further reading

Historians such as Samuel Johnson, whose *History of the Yorubas* was compiled in the nineteenth century, and later scholars such as Robin Law, in *The Oyo Empire c.1600 to c.1836*, provide detailed accounts of this history.
