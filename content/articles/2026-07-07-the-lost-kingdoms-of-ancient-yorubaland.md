## Introduction

The history of the Yoruba is often told through the stories of great kingdoms that continue to exist today, such as **Ile-Ife**, **Oyo**, **Ijebu Ode**, and **Akure**. However, the political landscape of ancient Yorubaland was once far more diverse. Numerous kingdoms rose to prominence, only to decline, fragment, relocate, or disappear over time.

These "lost kingdoms" were not necessarily forgotten. Many survive in oral traditions, archaeological remains, royal genealogies, or as communities that moved to new locations. Others were absorbed into larger states or transformed by war, migration, and changing trade routes.

Exploring these kingdoms provides a deeper understanding of the rise and transformation of Yoruba civilisation.

## What Does "Lost Kingdom" Mean?

In the context of Yoruba history, a lost kingdom does not always mean a civilisation that vanished completely.

A kingdom may be considered "lost" because:

* Its original capital was abandoned.
* It was conquered by another state.
* Its ruling dynasty relocated.
* It fragmented into smaller kingdoms.
* Its political influence declined significantly.
* Only archaeological or oral evidence remains of its former importance.

Many of these kingdoms continued to influence later Yoruba states even after their decline.

## Old Oyo (Oyo-Ile)

One of the best-known examples is **Old Oyo Empire**, whose original capital, Oyo-Ile, once governed one of Africa's largest empires.

During its height between the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, Old Oyo controlled extensive trade networks and exercised political influence across much of present-day south-western Nigeria and neighbouring regions.

In the early nineteenth century, internal political tensions, succession disputes, and external pressures, including conflicts linked to the Fulani expansion, led to the abandonment of Oyo-Ile. The political centre later shifted farther south, but the original capital survives today as an important archaeological site.

## Kétou

Kétou was one of the oldest Yoruba kingdoms and is closely associated with traditions linking its royal dynasty to Ile-Ife.

Throughout its history, Kétou experienced repeated invasions and periods of destruction because of its strategic location between powerful neighbouring states. Although the kingdom survived, its political influence declined compared with its earlier prominence.

Today, Kétou remains an important cultural centre preserving Yoruba traditions in present-day Benin.

## Old Ṣàbẹ́ (Savè)

The historic Yoruba kingdom of **Savè**, known in Yoruba as Ṣàbẹ́, played an important role in regional politics and trade.

Located in present-day Benin, the kingdom experienced conflicts with neighbouring powers over several centuries. While the kingdom still exists in cultural and traditional form, its earlier political significance diminished as colonial rule reshaped the region.

## Owu Kingdom

The **Owu Kingdom** was once among the most influential Yoruba states.

Its fortunes changed dramatically during the early nineteenth century when the devastating Owu War led to the destruction of its capital.

Many Owu people migrated to new settlements, including parts of present-day Abeokuta, where they rebuilt their communities and preserved their traditions. Although the original kingdom declined, the Owu identity remains strong today.

## Ancient Settlements Around Ilé-Ifẹ̀

Archaeologists have identified numerous early settlements around **Ile-Ife** that flourished before or alongside the emergence of the city's royal traditions.

Many of these communities were eventually incorporated into the expanding urban centre. Their individual names and political structures have largely been lost, but archaeological discoveries of pottery, ironworking sites, and domestic compounds demonstrate that they contributed to the growth of one of Africa's oldest urban civilisations.

## Smaller Kingdoms Absorbed by Larger States

Throughout Yoruba history, numerous small kingdoms emerged and disappeared.

Some were absorbed into larger states through:

* Political alliances.
* Dynastic unions.
* Warfare.
* Voluntary incorporation.
* Population movements.

In many cases, the names of these kingdoms survive only in oral traditions, local histories, or the titles of traditional rulers.

## Why Kingdoms Disappeared

Several factors contributed to the decline of ancient Yoruba kingdoms:

* Civil wars.
* External invasions.
* Changing trade routes.
* Population migration.
* Environmental pressures.
* Political succession disputes.
* The expansion of stronger neighbouring states.

The nineteenth-century Yoruba wars were especially significant, leading to the destruction, relocation, or transformation of many settlements.

## Archaeology and Oral Tradition

Much of what we know about these kingdoms comes from combining different sources.

Archaeology provides evidence of:

* Settlement patterns.
* Building remains.
* Pottery.
* Ironworking.
* Trade goods.

Oral traditions preserve:

* Royal genealogies.
* Migration stories.
* Founding legends.
* Historical memories.

Together, these sources help reconstruct the history of kingdoms that left few written records.

## Their Enduring Legacy

Although many ancient kingdoms declined or changed, their legacy remains visible.

Their influence can still be seen in:

* Traditional monarchies.
* Royal titles.
* Festivals.
* Family lineages.
* Local customs.
* Historic towns.
* Archaeological sites.

Many modern Yoruba communities continue to honour the histories of kingdoms whose political power has faded but whose cultural importance endures.

## Conclusion

The lost kingdoms of ancient Yorubaland remind us that history is a story of change as much as continuity. Great states such as the **Old Oyo Empire**, the **Owu Kingdom**, **Kétou**, and Ṣàbẹ́ experienced periods of remarkable influence before being reshaped by warfare, migration, political change, and shifting trade networks.

Rather than disappearing entirely, many of these kingdoms evolved into new communities, relocated their capitals, or preserved their traditions through royal families and oral history. Their stories enrich our understanding of Yoruba civilisation and demonstrate that the strength of a culture is not measured only by the survival of its political institutions, but also by the resilience of its people and the enduring legacy they pass from one generation to the next.
