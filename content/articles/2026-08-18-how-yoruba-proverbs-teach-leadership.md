## Introduction

Among the Yoruba, proverbs, known as **òwe**, have traditionally been far more than clever sayings. They are a form of condensed wisdom, used to teach people how to think, behave, make decisions, and relate to others.

Leadership is one of the areas where Yoruba proverbs are particularly powerful. Instead of giving a leader a long list of instructions, a short proverb can communicate an entire principle about **wisdom, patience, justice, humility, responsibility, consultation, and the consequences of abusing power**.

This made proverbs an important part of traditional political and social education.

## 1. Wisdom Is More Valuable Than Power

Yoruba ideas of leadership traditionally placed considerable importance on **ọgbọ́n**, wisdom.

A person could possess authority without possessing wisdom. A good leader needed to know when to speak, when to listen, when to act, and when to seek advice.

This principle is captured in the proverb:

> **Ọ̀rọ̀ tó bá yẹ kí a sọ, a kì í sọ ọ́ láìrò.**

The broader lesson is that important decisions should be considered carefully rather than made impulsively.

For a leader, intelligence is not simply knowing things. It is knowing **how and when to use knowledge**.

## 2. A Leader Must Listen

One of the strongest lessons in Yoruba political traditions is the importance of consultation.

Traditional rulers did not necessarily govern as isolated individuals. Councils of chiefs, elders, family heads, and other institutions could provide advice and participate in decision-making.

Proverbs reinforced the value of listening because no individual possesses all knowledge.

A leader who refuses advice can easily mistake personal opinion for collective wisdom.

## 3. Patience Is a Leadership Skill

The Yoruba concept of **sùúrù**, patience, appears repeatedly in traditional moral teaching.

Leadership inevitably involves difficult decisions, disagreements, and provocation. A leader who reacts immediately to every problem can create larger problems.

Proverbs therefore encourage restraint and thoughtful action.

The lesson is simple:

**A leader who controls their emotions is better positioned to control a difficult situation.**

## 4. A Leader Must Understand Consequences

Yoruba proverbs frequently remind listeners that actions have consequences.

This is particularly important for leaders because their decisions affect many other people.

A careless decision by an ordinary individual may affect a household. A careless decision by a ruler can affect an entire community.

Proverbs therefore encourage leaders to think beyond the immediate moment.

## 5. Humility Protects Leadership

A recurring Yoruba moral principle is that **pride can destroy good character**.

Leadership naturally creates status, but status can become dangerous when it produces arrogance.

A wise leader understands that authority is a responsibility rather than proof of personal superiority.

This is closely connected to **ìwà**, character. A person's title does not automatically make them admirable; their conduct does.

## 6. Justice Must Accompany Authority

Leadership without justice can quickly become oppression.

Traditional Yoruba communities developed mechanisms for settling disputes through elders, chiefs, family heads, and other authorities. Proverbs helped reinforce the expectation that disputes should be approached with fairness and wisdom.

A leader was expected to distinguish between:

* Personal interest and community interest.
* Anger and justice.
* Revenge and legitimate correction.
* Favouritism and fairness.

This is why the moral character of a leader mattered so much.

## 7. The Leader Is Responsible for the Community

Yoruba political thought traditionally understood leadership as a relationship involving obligations.

A ruler had responsibilities towards the people, just as subjects had responsibilities towards the political community.

Proverbs reinforced the idea that leadership should serve a wider purpose.

A leader who concentrates entirely on personal wealth or prestige eventually weakens the community that sustains their authority.

## 8. Collective Wisdom Is Stronger Than Individual Wisdom

One of the most important leadership lessons found in Yoruba traditions is the value of **collective decision-making**.

The proverb:

> **Ọ̀pọ̀ ọgbọ́n ni a fi ń dá ilé ayé.**

can be understood broadly as expressing the idea that many forms of wisdom are needed to manage human affairs.

The principle is particularly relevant to governance.

A leader can have authority while still recognising that other people possess knowledge they do not.

## 9. Good Leaders Know When to Speak

Yoruba proverbs frequently teach the importance of careful speech.

Words can:

* Resolve conflict.
* Create conflict.
* Build trust.
* Destroy relationships.
* Encourage people.
* Humiliate people.

For a leader, speech carries even greater weight because people pay attention to what leaders say.

A wise leader therefore does not simply ask, **"Can I say this?"**

They also consider:

**"Is this the right thing to say, at the right time, in the right way?"**

## 10. Reputation Is Part of Leadership

A leader's reputation was traditionally connected to the reputation of their family and institution.

This connects leadership to **ìwà**.

Someone who is dishonest, arrogant, unreliable, or unjust may possess a title, but their conduct can undermine their legitimacy.

Yoruba moral thought therefore places an important distinction between **having authority** and **being worthy of respect**.

## 11. Courage Does Not Mean Recklessness

Traditional leaders sometimes faced warfare, political disputes, and threats to their communities.

Proverbs could therefore distinguish genuine courage from reckless behaviour.

A courageous leader acts when action is necessary, but wisdom determines **how** and **when** to act.

The best leader is not necessarily the person who rushes towards every confrontation.

Sometimes leadership means knowing when conflict should be avoided.

## 12. Leadership Begins With Self-Discipline

Before someone can effectively lead others, Yoruba moral traditions emphasise the importance of controlling oneself.

A leader needs:

* Patience.
* Discipline.
* Honesty.
* Emotional control.
* Humility.
* Responsibility.

This reflects the broader concept of **Omolúàbí**, the ideal of a person whose character makes them worthy of trust and respect.

## 13. Proverbs Teach Through Indirection

One particularly interesting feature of Yoruba wisdom is that elders do not always state a lesson directly.

Instead of saying:

**"Do not be arrogant."**

a speaker might use a proverb that causes the listener to think about the consequences of arrogance.

This makes the listener participate in the lesson.

Understanding the proverb becomes part of the learning process.

That is one reason Yoruba proverbs are so effective in teaching leadership: **they develop judgement rather than merely issuing commands.**

## 14. Proverbs Could Correct Powerful People

Perhaps one of the most interesting aspects of proverbs is that they could be used to communicate criticism without direct confrontation.

In a hierarchical society, telling a powerful person bluntly that they were wrong could be difficult.

A carefully selected proverb could make the same point indirectly.

This gave elders and advisers a culturally recognised way to challenge behaviour while maintaining social decorum.

## 15. Leadership Was About Legacy

Yoruba historical traditions frequently preserve the names and actions of rulers long after their deaths.

This creates an important leadership lesson:

**Power is temporary, but reputation can outlive the person who held it.**

A ruler's legacy could be remembered through:

* Oral histories.
* Praise poetry.
* Proverbs.
* Songs.
* Genealogies.
* Palace traditions.

A leader therefore had to consider how future generations would remember their actions.

## Proverbs as a Traditional Leadership School

Taken together, Yoruba proverbs functioned almost like an informal school of leadership.

They taught that a good leader should:

**Listen before deciding.**\
**Think before speaking.**\
**Control anger.**\
**Seek wisdom from others.**\
**Treat people fairly.**\
**Avoid arrogance.**\
**Accept responsibility.**\
**Think about consequences.**\
**Protect the community.**\
**Build a good reputation.**

These principles were not confined to kings. They could apply to parents, family heads, elders, businesspeople, community organisers, and anyone responsible for others.

## Conclusion

Yoruba proverbs provided a sophisticated way of teaching leadership without turning every lesson into a formal lecture. Through metaphor, humour, irony, observation, and accumulated experience, **òwe** taught people how authority should be exercised.

The central message is remarkably consistent: **true leadership is not measured simply by how much power someone possesses, but by how wisely, fairly, and responsibly they use it.**

That is why Yoruba proverbs remain relevant beyond traditional kingdoms. Their lessons about listening, patience, humility, justice, self-control, consultation, and responsibility apply just as easily to a traditional ruler as they do to a parent, teacher, community leader, or modern organisation.
