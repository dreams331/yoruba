Water has always been essential to human settlement. Communities need it for drinking, cooking, farming, sanitation, craft production and religious practices. For the Yoruba, managing water was therefore an important part of settlement life.

Long before modern boreholes, water treatment plants and piped networks, Yoruba communities relied on a combination of natural water sources and locally developed methods of collecting, storing and accessing water. Archaeological evidence from places such as **Ilé Ifẹ̀ and Old Ọ̀yọ́** provides valuable evidence of how water was incorporated into settlement design.

Although the surviving evidence does not allow us to reconstruct one single water management system used throughout Yorùbáland, it shows that Yoruba communities developed practical responses to their local environments.

## Wells as Sources of Groundwater

One of the simplest ways communities could obtain water was by accessing groundwater through wells.

Archaeological and historical evidence records wells among the features found at Old Ọ̀yọ́. UNESCO's description of the archaeological site mentions wells, cisterns and a former water reservoir within the extensive settlement.

Traditional wells were particularly valuable because they could provide access to groundwater when surface water sources became less reliable.

Research into southwestern Nigeria has also documented the traditional use of springs and wells to access shallow groundwater in areas underlain by basement complex rocks. Oyo State is specifically included in this research.

The existence of wells demonstrates an important principle of indigenous water management: communities learned to work with the geological characteristics of the land rather than depending entirely on surface streams and rivers.

## Reservoirs and Water Storage

Archaeological evidence from Old Ọ̀yọ́ provides one of the clearest examples of organised water storage in a Yoruba urban settlement.

UNESCO records the remains of a past water reservoir within the Old Ọ̀yọ́ site. Archaeological fieldwork has also documented a dried up water reservoir among the features mapped at the site.

A reservoir would have been particularly valuable in a large settlement because water availability could change with the seasons. Storing water created a reserve that could remain useful beyond the period when water was immediately available.

This is significant because Old Ọ̀yọ́ was not simply a small farming village. It developed into a major political centre, meaning that managing essential resources was an important part of sustaining urban life.

## Rainwater and the Natural Landscape

Rainfall was another important source of water.

The Yoruba homeland receives substantial seasonal rainfall, but rain does not fall evenly throughout the year. Communities therefore needed ways of making use of water when it was available and finding reliable sources during drier periods.

Traditional societies across Nigeria developed different forms of rainwater collection and storage. Research on indigenous water management in Nigeria records practices including rainwater harvesting, natural springs and hand dug wells.

For Yoruba communities, the surrounding landscape itself was also important. Valleys, streams, springs and low lying areas could provide access to water, while settlement location could influence how easily people reached these sources.

## Potsherd Pavements and Water Control

One of the most fascinating examples of Yoruba indigenous technology is the use of **potsherd pavements**.

These pavements were constructed from broken pottery fragments, sometimes combined with stones or pebbles. Archaeological research has found them in places including palaces, shrines and household courtyards.

They were not simply decorative.

Recent archaeological research identifies their functional roles in **water management, erosion control and sanitation**. The distinctive herringbone and edge laid patterns are particularly associated with classical Ilé Ifẹ̀ and later became widespread across parts of Yorùbáland.

This is an important reminder that discarded materials could be given a new purpose. Broken pottery, which might otherwise have been waste, could become part of durable architectural surfaces.

## Protecting Buildings from Water and Erosion

Heavy seasonal rainfall can damage buildings and wash away exposed soil.

Paved surfaces provided a stronger alternative to bare earth around important buildings and courtyards. Potsherd pavements could help manage the movement of water across these spaces while reducing erosion.

Archaeological research has identified erosion control alongside water management and sanitation as one of the practical functions of these pavements.

This shows that indigenous architecture was not only concerned with appearance. Building materials and surface design could also respond to environmental conditions.

## Water and Yoruba Sacred Traditions

Water had significance beyond its practical value.

Rivers, springs and wells could have religious and cultural importance within Yoruba communities. Some water bodies became associated with particular Òrìṣà and local traditions.

Research from the University of Ilorin, for example, examines the historical and religious significance of sacred water bodies and holy wells in Yorùbáland. It specifically discusses the Olókun water body in Ilé Ifẹ̀ and Orí Ayé in Ondo.

The famous **Odò Ọ̀ṣun at Ọ̀ṣogbo** is another example of the deep relationship between water, spirituality and Yoruba cultural heritage. The river remains central to the annual Ọ̀ṣun Ọ̀ṣogbo Festival and the cultural landscape surrounding the sacred grove.

This relationship means that water could be simultaneously a practical resource, a natural feature and an important part of religious life.

## Water and Settlement Planning

Access to water could influence where people established settlements.

Archaeological studies of Yoruba settlements show that communities occupied different environments, including lowlands, hills and areas around rock formations. Settlement patterns reflected responses to environmental and social conditions.

This does not mean that every Yoruba settlement was deliberately designed around a formal water engineering plan. Rather, communities adapted their settlement choices to the landscapes available to them.

Where water was accessible, it could support domestic activities, agriculture and craft production. Where it was scarce, communities had greater reason to develop methods for accessing or storing it.

## Water Management in Old Ọ̀yọ́

Old Ọ̀yọ́ provides particularly valuable evidence because of its scale.

The archaeological site contains extensive walls, settlement remains, wells and a former reservoir. UNESCO describes the former city as a large settlement enclosed by extensive defensive walls and associated features.

The presence of water infrastructure within such a large settlement demonstrates that access to water was an important practical concern.

However, it would be inaccurate to describe Old Ọ̀yọ́ as having a modern style central water supply system. The surviving evidence points instead to a combination of wells, stored water and natural resources that supported life within the settlement.

## Indigenous Knowledge and Environmental Understanding

Traditional water management required detailed knowledge of the environment.

People needed to understand where water could be found, how seasonal rainfall affected availability, which locations were suitable for wells and how water moved across the landscape.

This knowledge was often practical and transmitted through experience rather than through written engineering manuals.

It formed part of a broader indigenous knowledge system involving farming, architecture, pottery, settlement planning and environmental observation.

## What the Archaeological Evidence Tells Us

The surviving evidence gives us a fascinating picture, but it also has limits.

We have archaeological evidence for wells, reservoirs and potsherd pavements. We also have historical and ethnographic evidence concerning springs, wells and sacred water bodies.

What we do not have is sufficient evidence to claim that every Yoruba kingdom possessed an identical, centrally organised water management system.

Yoruba communities lived across different environments, from forest regions to savannah areas. Their methods naturally varied according to geography, settlement size, available resources and historical circumstances.

This distinction is important because respecting Yoruba history also means respecting the evidence.

## A Legacy of Environmental Intelligence

Ancient Yoruba water management demonstrates the ability of communities to adapt technology to their environment.

Wells provided access to groundwater. Reservoirs created opportunities for water storage. Natural springs and other water sources supported settlements. Potsherd pavements helped control water, reduce erosion and improve sanitation.

These practices were not isolated inventions. They formed part of a wider culture of observation, craftsmanship and adaptation.

Today, modern water infrastructure has transformed how communities obtain and distribute water. Yet the older knowledge remains valuable because it shows how Yoruba societies understood their environment and created practical solutions with the materials available to them.

The story of Yoruba water management is therefore not simply a story about wells and reservoirs. It is a story about **adaptation, environmental knowledge and indigenous technology**.

It reminds us that technological knowledge did not begin with modern machines. Long before modern infrastructure, Yoruba communities were already finding ways to work with the land, rainfall, groundwater and built environment to support human life.
