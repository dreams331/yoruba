## Ìrẹtẹ̀-Ọ̀yẹ̀kú

### Overview

Patience is inherited. The stamina to carry on through hard times often comes from the example of earlier generations.

### Governing Influences

- **Ìrẹtẹ̀ Méjì** — perseverance despite setbacks, hidden victory (Ọ̀rúnmìlà / Ọya)
- **Ọ̀yẹ̀kú Méjì** — endings, the ancestors, the unseen world, mortality (Ikú (Death) / Ọ̀sun)


### Guidance

Draw strength from those who endured before you.

<!-- READING_START -->

### How this figure is read

In Ifá, a figure is built from two columns of four marks, read from the right column to the left. Here the right column is Ìrẹtẹ̀ Méjì (single · single · double · single) and the left column is Ọ̀yẹ̀kú Méjì (double · double · double · double). Many diviners start with the right column and then bring in the left, though schools of Ifá differ in how they weigh the two. Reading Ìrẹtẹ̀-Ọ̀yẹ̀kú means holding both lessons together and asking how they bear on the question at hand.

### From Ìrẹtẹ̀ Méjì

Ìrẹtẹ̀ teaches resilience in adversity, especially struggles others cannot see. It reassures that persistence — even quiet, unglamorous effort — leads to eventual triumph.

*Ìṣẹ́gun tí a kò fojú rí ni í dùn jù.*  
"The victory unseen by others is often the sweetest."

### From Ọ̀yẹ̀kú Méjì

Ọ̀yẹ̀kú teaches respect for the ancestors, acceptance of mortality, and the understanding that every ending clears space for renewal. It warns against arrogance in the face of life's fragility.

*Òkú tó sun, kì í jí mọ́; ẹ̀mí tó lọ, kì í padà bí ara.*  
"The dead that sleeps does not wake again; the spirit that departs does not return in the same body."

### Putting the two together

This figure reads as reassurance for those who feel stuck. Keep going, keep records of progress, and avoid measuring yourself against people whose circumstances differ. Results often arrive later than hoped but are lasting when earned. At the same time, read in daily life, this figure asks a person to honour those who came before, settle unfinished obligations, and stop treating limits as enemies. It cautions against pride about health, wealth or status, since all of them are borrowed for a time.

Interpretations of individual Omo Odù differ between lineages and between diviners, so treat this page as a reading aid and not a ruling. A consultation with a trained Babaláwo or Ìyánífá will always be fuller than any written summary.
