## Ogbè-Ọ̀yẹ̀kú

### Overview

Light meets the threshold of the ancestors. This figure speaks of a bright beginning that must be built on respect for those who came before, and of potential that is only realised when it is rooted in lineage.

### Governing Influences

* **Èjì Ogbè** — light, new beginnings, divine potential (Ọ̀rúnmìlà / Olódùmarè)
* **Ọ̀yẹ̀kú Méjì** — endings, the ancestors, the unseen world, mortality (Ikú (Death) / Ọ̀sun)


### Guidance

Honour your ancestors before launching a new venture, and let gratitude steady your ambition.

<!-- READING_START -->

### How this figure is read

In Ifá, a figure is built from two columns of four marks, read from the right column to the left. Here the right column is Èjì Ogbè (single · single · single · single) and the left column is Ọ̀yẹ̀kú Méjì (double · double · double · double). Many diviners start with the right column and then bring in the left, though schools of Ifá differ in how they weigh the two. Reading Ogbè-Ọ̀yẹ̀kú means holding both lessons together and asking how they bear on the question at hand.

### From Èjì Ogbè

You were not born by accident. Every person carries an ori (destiny) chosen before birth. Ogbè teaches that your task is to remember and fulfil your purpose, and to lead with clarity, honesty and generosity of spirit.

*Imọ́lẹ̀ kìí ṣe n̄ǹkan tí a fún ọ — imọ́lẹ̀ ni ọ jẹ́.*  
"Light is not something given to you — light is what you are."

### From Ọ̀yẹ̀kú Méjì

Ọ̀yẹ̀kú teaches respect for the ancestors, acceptance of mortality, and the understanding that every ending clears space for renewal. It warns against arrogance in the face of life's fragility.

*Òkú tó sun, kì í jí mọ́; ẹ̀mí tó lọ, kì í padà bí ara.*  
"The dead that sleeps does not wake again; the spirit that departs does not return in the same body."

### Putting the two together

When this figure is cast, tradition reads it as encouragement to act openly. Plan in the light, speak plainly, and avoid shortcuts that need to be hidden later. It also reminds a person that a good start still needs steady follow-through. At the same time, read in daily life, this figure asks a person to honour those who came before, settle unfinished obligations, and stop treating limits as enemies. It cautions against pride about health, wealth or status, since all of them are borrowed for a time.

Interpretations of individual Omo Odù differ between lineages and between diviners, so treat this page as a reading aid and not a ruling. A consultation with a trained Babaláwo or Ìyánífá will always be fuller than any written summary.
