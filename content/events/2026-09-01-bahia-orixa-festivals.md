In Bahia, Brazil, the descendants of enslaved Yoruba people have preserved and evolved Yoruba spiritual traditions through Candomblé — a religion centered on the Orixás (the Brazilian-Portuguese rendering of Òrìṣà).

## Cultural Connection

Terreiros (Candomblé temple communities) throughout Bahia — particularly in Salvador — hold regular festivals and ceremonies honoring Orixás such as Ọ̀ṣun (Oxum), Ṣàngó (Xangô), Yemọja (Iemanjá), and Ògún (Ogum), preserving Yoruba language fragments, songs, and rites passed down for generations.

## Notable Celebrations

- **Festa de Iemanjá** (Festival of Yemọja) — celebrated by fishing communities along Bahia's coast
- Regular terreiro ceremonies open to respectful visitors and researchers of the diaspora

## Why It Matters

These traditions represent one of the most powerful examples of Yoruba cultural survival and adaptation across the Atlantic, connecting millions of Afro-Brazilians to their ancestral Yoruba heritage.

## Salvador and the Terreiros

Salvador was a major arrival point for enslaved Africans, and Yoruba speakers, known in Brazil as the Nagô, shaped the city's religious life. Candomblé houses, called terreiros, grew from the nineteenth century. Casa Branca do Engenho Velho is often cited as the oldest, and Ilê Axé Opô Afonjá, founded in the early twentieth century, is one of the best known. Many follow the Ketu tradition, which uses Yoruba words in its songs, greetings and prayers.

## Festivals Open to the Public

The best known public event is the Festa de Iemanjá on February 2 at Rio Vermelho in Salvador. Devotees and fishing families bring flowers, perfume and small gifts to the sea, and the offerings are carried out in boats. In January, the Lavagem do Bonfim sees participants wash the steps of the Bonfim church in a procession that blends Catholic and Candomblé customs, with the Orixá Oxalá (Ọbàtálá) linked to the Lord of Bonfim. Most inner ceremonies of a terreiro are not public, and invitations come through the house itself.

## Visiting Respectfully

Visitors should ask permission before attending any ceremony, dress in plain clothing, and avoid recording without consent. Some rooms and moments are closed to outsiders. Local guides and museums, including the Afro-Brazilian Museum in Salvador, give useful background without intruding on worship.
