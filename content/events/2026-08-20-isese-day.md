Ìṣẹ̀ṣe Day (also called African Traditional Religion Day) is observed on August 20 each year across Lagos, Ọ̀ṣun, Ọ̀yọ́, Ògùn, Ondo, and Ekiti states, celebrating indigenous Yoruba spirituality and heritage.

## Purpose

The day was instituted to give recognition and respect to adherents of Ìṣẹ̀ṣe (traditional Yoruba religious practice), who for generations faced marginalization compared to Christianity and Islam. It's now marked with public holidays in several states.

## Celebrations

- Colorful processions through major cities including Lagos
- Traditional worship and libations to the Òrìṣà
- Cultural displays of drumming, dance, and attire
- Participation from Ifá priests (Babaláwo), Ìyánífá, and traditional worshippers of Ṣàngó, Ọya, Ọ̀ṣun, and other deities

## Diaspora Connection

Communities in Brazil, Cuba, Trinidad, and the United States that practice Yoruba-derived religions (Candomblé, Santería/Lucumí, Vodou-adjacent traditions) often hold parallel observances.

## Why the Day Exists

For much of the twentieth century, Yoruba traditional religion was spoken of as something old-fashioned, and worshippers were often treated with suspicion by their neighbours and sometimes by their own relatives. Practitioners, scholars and cultural groups argued that Ìṣẹ̀ṣe, which means "origin" or "tradition", should stand on equal footing with the two religions that came to dominate the region. Ìṣẹ̀ṣe Day grew from that campaign, and several Southwest states have since recognised August 20 as a holiday.

## What You Might See

The mood is public and relaxed. Groups of priests and priestesses in white or in the colours of their deities walk through town squares. There are drummers, praise-singers and chanters of *ẹ̀sẹ̀ Ifá*, the verses of the Ifá corpus. Families who follow the tradition pour libation at home and share food with neighbours. Some towns hold lectures and school programmes on Yoruba names, proverbs and history.

## Debates Around It

Not everyone welcomes the day. Some Christian and Muslim leaders have objected to the holiday, while some practitioners say the official events present a simplified picture of a varied religion. Both views are part of the picture, and anyone writing about the day should cover them fairly. The day is best understood as a public statement that traditional belief is part of Yoruba life and deserves the same legal and social respect as any other faith.
