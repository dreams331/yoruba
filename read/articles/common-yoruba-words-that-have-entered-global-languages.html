Common Yoruba Words That Have Entered Global Languages

June 1, 2026 Yoruba Heritage Team

Introduction

Languages rarely remain confined to the places where they originate. Through migration, trade, religion, literature, music, and cultural exchange, words travel across continents and become part of new vocabularies. The Yoruba language is no exception.

As one of Africa's most influential languages, Yoruba has left a lasting mark far beyond Nigeria, Benin, and Togo. Through the transatlantic slave trade and the resilience of Yoruba descendants in the diaspora, many Yoruba words survived and became integrated into languages spoken in Cuba, Brazil, the Caribbean, the Americas, and even global popular culture.

Some of these words are still recognized in forms very close to their original Yoruba pronunciation, while others have evolved over time. Together, they tell a remarkable story of cultural survival and global influence.

How Yoruba Words Traveled Around the World

Between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries, large numbers of Yoruba people were forcibly taken to the Americas during the transatlantic slave trade.

Despite immense hardship, they preserved elements of their language, religion, music, and traditions. Over generations, Yoruba vocabulary became embedded in religious practices such as:

* Santería in Cuba * Candomblé in Brazil * Various Afro-Caribbean spiritual traditions

As these traditions developed, Yoruba words continued to be spoken, sung, and passed from one generation to another.

Orisha

One of the most internationally recognized Yoruba words is Orisha.

In Yoruba, Òrìṣà refers to divine beings or spiritual forces associated with various aspects of nature and human existence.

Today, the word "Orisha" is widely used throughout:

* Cuba * Brazil * Trinidad * The United States * Academic studies of African religions

It has become a global term for Yoruba-derived spiritual traditions.

Ashe

Perhaps no Yoruba word has achieved broader international recognition than Ashe.

Derived from the Yoruba word Àṣẹ, it refers to spiritual power, authority, divine energy, or the ability to make things happen.

The concept is central to Yoruba philosophy and spirituality.

Today, "Ashe" is commonly used in:

* Afro-Caribbean religions * Afro-Brazilian traditions * Spiritual communities worldwide * Academic discussions of African religions

Many people use the word as a blessing or affirmation similar to "amen."

Shango

The name Shango originates from the Yoruba deity Ṣàngó, who is associated with thunder, lightning, justice, and royal power.

Through diaspora traditions, Shango became known across the Caribbean and the Americas.

Today, the name appears in:

* Religious ceremonies * Literature * Music * Popular culture * Academic research

Its continued use demonstrates the global reach of Yoruba spiritual heritage.

Ogun

The word Ogun has also traveled widely.

Ogun is the Yoruba deity associated with iron, craftsmanship, labor, technology, and warfare.

The name remains recognizable throughout numerous African diaspora traditions and has appeared in books, films, academic texts, and cultural studies around the world.

Babalawo

The Yoruba term Babaláwo means "father of wisdom" or "father of secrets."

It refers to a priest of the Ifá divination system.

Today, the word is used internationally among practitioners of Ifá and related traditions in:

* Nigeria * Cuba * Brazil * Venezuela * The United States * Other diaspora communities

Unlike many borrowed words, Babaláwo has largely retained its original meaning.

Ifá

The word Ifá has become internationally recognized among scholars, religious practitioners, and cultural historians.

The global spread of Ifá has introduced Yoruba philosophical concepts into discussions about spirituality, ethics, destiny, and indigenous knowledge systems.

Today, Ifá is studied and practiced on multiple continents.

Eshu and Elegua

The Yoruba deity Eshu became known in Cuba and other parts of the diaspora as Elegua or Elegguá.

Although the pronunciation evolved, the linguistic roots remain clearly Yoruba.

The name remains central in Afro-Cuban religious traditions and is recognized internationally among practitioners and researchers.

Obeah and Possible Yoruba Connections

Some scholars have suggested that certain Caribbean words may contain influences from Yoruba and other West African languages.

One often discussed example is "Obeah," a term associated with spiritual practices in parts of the Caribbean.

The exact linguistic origins remain debated, and researchers have proposed multiple African sources. While Yoruba influence may be part of the discussion, no single origin has been universally accepted.

This illustrates how African languages blended and evolved within diaspora communities.

Yoruba Words in Music and Popular Culture

In recent decades, Yoruba words have reached even broader audiences through:

* Afrobeats music * Literature * Film * Television * Academic scholarship * Social media

Artists frequently use Yoruba expressions, greetings, and spiritual terms in songs heard around the world.

Words such as:

* Àṣẹ * Oríkì * Orisha * Ifá * Ṣàngó

are increasingly familiar to audiences far beyond Yorubaland.

Language as Cultural Memory

The survival of Yoruba words in global languages represents more than linguistic borrowing.

Each word carries fragments of:

* History * Spirituality * Identity * Memory * Cultural resilience

For descendants of Yoruba people throughout the diaspora, these words often serve as living connections to ancestral heritage.

They remind communities that language can survive even the most difficult historical circumstances.

Conclusion

The influence of Yoruba on global languages reflects the remarkable journey of a people whose culture transcended oceans and generations. Through religion, music, scholarship, and cultural exchange, Yoruba words such as Orisha, Àṣẹ, Ifá, Babaláwo, Ogun, and Shango have become part of international vocabulary.

These words are more than linguistic artifacts. They are living evidence of the endurance of Yoruba civilization and its contribution to world culture.

Every time one of these words is spoken in Havana, Salvador, New York, London, Lagos, or anywhere else in the world, it carries with it a piece of Yoruba history, reminding us that languages have the power to preserve identity across centuries and continents.
