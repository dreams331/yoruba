How Princes Were Trained in Ancient Yoruba Kingdoms

July 7, 2026 Yoruba Heritage Team

Introduction

In ancient Yorubaland, becoming a king was not simply a matter of birth. While princes were born into royal families, they were expected to undergo years of education, discipline, and practical experience before they could assume positions of leadership. A future ruler needed to understand governance, diplomacy, military affairs, religion, law, and the customs of the people.

Training began early in life and continued into adulthood. Princes learned not only how to rule but also how to serve their communities, preserve tradition, and uphold the dignity of the royal institution. Although practices varied among kingdoms such as Ile-Ife, the Old Oyo Empire, Ijebu Ode, and Owo, many aspects of royal education were widely shared.

Birth Into Royal Responsibility

From birth, princes were raised with the understanding that they carried responsibilities as members of the royal household.

They were expected to:

* Demonstrate good character. * Respect elders. * Learn family history. * Preserve royal traditions. * Serve as examples to others.

Not every prince would become king, but all were expected to contribute to the welfare and reputation of their dynasty.

Learning Yoruba History and Oral Tradition

A prince's education began with history.

Royal children learned about:

* The origins of their kingdom. * The genealogy of their royal family. * Important past rulers. * Migration traditions. * Famous battles. * Alliances with neighbouring kingdoms.

Much of this knowledge was preserved through oral tradition and taught by palace elders, court historians, and respected members of the royal household.

Education in Governance

Princes were introduced to government by observing the daily activities of the palace.

They learned about:

* Decision-making. * Public administration. * Conflict resolution. * Taxation. * Diplomacy. * Relations with chiefs.

As they grew older, they often attended meetings of the king and council, gaining first-hand experience of how the kingdom was governed.

Learning Law and Justice

Future rulers were expected to understand customary law.

Princes studied how disputes were resolved through:

* Family mediation. * Councils of elders. * Chiefs. * Palace courts.

They learned that justice required fairness, patience, and respect for established customs rather than arbitrary decisions.

Military Training

Military education formed an important part of royal training, particularly in kingdoms that frequently faced conflict.

Princes were taught:

* Horse riding where appropriate. * Archery. * Spear handling. * Swordsmanship. * Hunting. * Leadership in times of war.

The Old Oyo Empire, famous for its cavalry, placed particular emphasis on military organisation and leadership.

Religious and Spiritual Education

Religion was closely connected to governance.

Princes were taught:

* Royal rituals. * Traditional festivals. * Respect for the Òrìṣà. * The importance of ancestors. * Ethical values preserved through Ifá teachings.

Many rulers regularly consulted priests and diviners before making important decisions, so princes needed to understand these traditions even if they did not become religious specialists themselves.

Public Speaking and Diplomacy

Communication was an essential leadership skill.

Princes learned how to:

* Speak respectfully. * Address councils. * Negotiate disputes. * Welcome visitors. * Represent the kingdom in diplomatic meetings.

The ability to communicate clearly and wisely was regarded as a mark of maturity and leadership.

Learning from the People

Royal education extended beyond the palace.

Princes were encouraged to observe the daily lives of:

* Farmers. * Hunters. * Traders. * Craftsmen. * Religious leaders. * Ordinary citizens.

Understanding the needs of different groups helped prepare future rulers to govern with wisdom and empathy.

Character Above Birth

Perhaps the most important lesson taught to princes was that noble birth alone did not make a good ruler.

Qualities expected of future kings included:

* Humility. * Courage. * Patience. * Honesty. * Generosity. * Self-discipline. * Respect for tradition.

In many kingdoms, councils of chiefs played an important role in selecting a ruler from eligible princes, meaning that personal character and competence could influence who ultimately ascended the throne.

The Selection of a King

When a king died, succession did not always pass automatically to the eldest son.

Depending on the customs of the kingdom, eligible princes from recognised royal lineages could be considered. Kingmakers, chiefs, and other traditional authorities evaluated candidates according to established succession rules, personal conduct, and the needs of the kingdom.

This system encouraged princes to prepare themselves throughout their lives, knowing that leadership required more than royal ancestry.

Legacy of Royal Education

Although modern education has transformed the preparation of traditional rulers, many royal families continue to value lessons in history, culture, public service, and leadership.

Traditional institutions still encourage future rulers to understand the customs and responsibilities associated with their offices, preserving aspects of a system that has evolved over centuries.

Conclusion

The training of princes in ancient Yoruba kingdoms reflected a deep understanding that effective leadership required knowledge, discipline, and service. From learning oral history and customary law to studying diplomacy, military strategy, religion, and public administration, royal education prepared future rulers to govern with wisdom and integrity.

Kingdoms such as Ile-Ife, the Old Oyo Empire, and Ijebu Ode recognised that the strength of a kingdom depended not only on its institutions but also on the character of those who led them. By combining practical experience with moral instruction and cultural knowledge, the Yoruba created a tradition of royal education that contributed to the longevity and stability of their political systems for many centuries.
