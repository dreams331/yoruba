The Kiriji War: The Longest Yoruba Civil War

July 2, 2026 Yoruba Heritage Team

Introduction

The Kiriji War, also known as the Ekiti-Parapo War, was the longest and one of the most devastating conflicts in Yoruba history. Fought between 1877 and 1893, the war lasted for sixteen years and involved some of the most powerful Yoruba states of the nineteenth century.

Far more than a series of battles, the Kiriji War represented a struggle over political authority, regional independence, and the future of Yorubaland following the collapse of the Old Oyo Empire. Entire communities were displaced, trade was disrupted, and thousands of lives were affected. At the same time, the conflict revealed the resilience of the Yoruba people and eventually led to important efforts towards peace and unity.

Today, the Kiriji War remains a defining chapter in Yoruba history and an important lesson about the human cost of prolonged conflict.

The Background to the War

The roots of the Kiriji War lay in the political changes that followed the decline of the Old Oyo Empire.

As Oyo's authority weakened during the nineteenth century, several new Yoruba states emerged, each seeking to protect its own interests.

Among the most influential was Ibadan, which had grown into a powerful military state through its victories in earlier Yoruba conflicts.

Many neighbouring kingdoms, however, became increasingly dissatisfied with Ibadan's dominance.

These included:

* Ekiti kingdoms. * Ijesa kingdoms. * Igbomina communities. * Akoko towns. * Other eastern Yoruba states.

Together, many of these communities later formed a military alliance known as the Ekiti-Parapo Confederacy.

Why the War Began

Several factors contributed to the outbreak of war.

These included:

* Opposition to Ibadan's political influence. * Disputes over tribute and taxation. * Competition for trade routes. * The desire for greater regional independence. * Long-standing political grievances.

The conflict was therefore not simply a struggle between two towns but a contest over the future political organisation of Yorubaland.

Why It Was Called the "Kiriji" War

The name "Kiriji" is believed to have come from the loud explosive sound made by newly introduced artillery during the fighting.

Witnesses described the booming noise of cannon fire as resembling the sound "ki-ri-ji."

Although firearms had appeared in earlier conflicts, the Kiriji War saw much greater use of modern weapons than previous Yoruba wars.

This marked an important turning point in military history across the region.

The Main Opposing Forces

The principal opponents were:

The Ibadan Army

Led by experienced military commanders, Ibadan sought to maintain its political influence over much of Yorubaland.

The Ekiti-Parapo Confederacy

This alliance brought together several eastern Yoruba kingdoms determined to resist Ibadan's authority and preserve their independence.

Although alliances shifted at different stages of the war, these two coalitions remained the central participants throughout most of the conflict.

Sixteen Years of Conflict

Unlike many earlier wars that ended after decisive battles, the Kiriji War became a prolonged struggle.

For sixteen years:

* Major offensives alternated with periods of stalemate. * Fortified camps became common. * Supply lines grew increasingly important. * Communities suffered repeated disruption. * Agricultural production declined in many areas.

Neither side achieved a decisive victory despite years of fighting.

The Human Cost

The effects of the war extended far beyond the battlefield.

Many people experienced:

* Displacement from their homes. * Food shortages. * Economic hardship. * Separation of families. * Interrupted trade. * Loss of farmland.

Markets declined in many regions, and numerous settlements were abandoned or severely damaged.

The conflict demonstrated how prolonged warfare affects ordinary people as much as soldiers.

The Peace Agreement

By the early 1890s, both sides had become exhausted by years of fighting.

Peace negotiations were encouraged through the efforts of Yoruba leaders as well as representatives of the British colonial administration, whose influence along the coast had been growing steadily.

The conflict formally ended in 1893, bringing sixteen years of warfare to a close.

Although peace was restored, many political and economic challenges remained.

Consequences of the War

The Kiriji War had lasting consequences.

It:

* Weakened many Yoruba states. * Reduced economic activity. * Encouraged greater British political involvement. * Brought an end to large-scale inter-Yoruba warfare. * Changed regional political relationships.

Within only a few years of the peace agreement, most Yoruba kingdoms came under British colonial administration.

In this sense, the end of the Kiriji War also marked the closing chapter of independent military rivalry among the major Yoruba states.

Lessons from the Kiriji War

The Kiriji War offers enduring lessons that remain relevant today.

It reminds us that:

* Political divisions can have devastating consequences. * Long wars rarely produce lasting winners. * Dialogue often achieves what violence cannot. * Unity strengthens communities. * Peace is essential for economic and cultural development.

The conflict also highlights the resilience of Yoruba communities, which rebuilt their towns, restored trade, and preserved their shared cultural identity despite years of hardship.

Conclusion

The Kiriji War was the longest civil war in Yoruba history, lasting from 1877 to 1893 and reshaping the political future of Yorubaland. Born out of disputes over power, independence, and regional influence, it involved major Yoruba states in a conflict that exhausted armies, displaced communities, and disrupted economic life for more than a decade. Although no side achieved a decisive military victory, the war ultimately paved the way for peace negotiations and brought an end to an era of large-scale inter-Yoruba warfare.

Its legacy extends far beyond the battlefield. The Kiriji War reminds us of the heavy cost of prolonged conflict, the importance of dialogue, and the enduring strength of a people who, despite years of division, preserved their shared heritage and rebuilt their society. It remains one of the most significant and instructive chapters in the history of the Yoruba nation.
