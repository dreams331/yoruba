The Warrior Who Could Not Die

October 12, 2025 Yoruba Heritage Team

The Prayer at the Forge

Agiri-Asasa was the greatest warrior Oyo had ever known. In forty battles, he had never been wounded. In twenty years of service, he had never lost a single man under his command. His enemies composed songs about him — not songs of hatred, but of awe — because there is a point at which a man becomes so formidable that even his enemies respect him.

But Agiri-Asasa was afraid of one thing: dying before his work was done.

He went to the sacred forge at the edge of the city where the priests of Ogun — the Orisha of iron, war, and the forge — kept an eternal flame. He fasted for seven days. He brought an iron blade he had forged himself, a white dog, and a bottle of palm wine.

On the eighth day, Ogun's presence settled over the forge like heat from a coal. Agiri-Asasa made his request: "Let me live until the last enemy of Oyo is defeated. Let no blade, no sickness, no fire touch me until my work is finished."

Ogun's priests consulted the ash and the flame. Then they spoke: "Ogun grants your wish. But understand: this prayer has no end clause. You said until the last enemy is defeated. As long as Oyo has enemies, you cannot die."

Agiri-Asasa smiled. He had no intention of dying.

---

The Centuries Pass

He fought brilliantly. He drove back every invader, suppressed every rebellion, and walked through fire and iron unharmed. He watched three Alaafins crowned and buried. He watched his own children grow old and die. He attended the funerals of grandchildren and great-grandchildren — faces he barely recognised, children who called him Baba Agba with a kind of frightened wonder.

Decades passed. Then generations. Then centuries.

Oyo fell and was rebuilt. Fell again. The world changed beyond recognition. Horses gave way to strange loud machines. The forest retreated. New towns grew up overnight. Languages shifted. And Agiri-Asasa walked among it all, unaging, unable to rest.

He could no longer find joy in the forge, in battle, in food, in music. He had outlived every friend, every love, every context that gave his life meaning. He was like a word from an old language — still existing, but no longer understood.

---

The Return to the Forge

Finally, Agiri-Asasa returned to where the old forge had been. It was a market now. He sat in the dust of it and wept for the first time in two hundred years.

A young girl selling groundnuts watched him. She had never seen an old man cry like that — with his whole body, the way children cry.

"What is wrong, Baba?" she asked.

"I forgot to ask for something important," he said. "I asked to live until my enemies were defeated. But I forgot to ask for people to live alongside. I forgot to ask for a reason to get up in the mornings."

The girl sat beside him. "Then start there," she said. "Find a reason."

He looked at her. She was perhaps twelve. She had her whole life ahead of her and she was already giving him advice. He laughed — quietly, rustily, like a door that hadn't been opened in years.

It was the laugh that finally brought Ogun back.

The Orisha appeared in the form of a blacksmith passing through the market. He put his hand briefly on Agiri-Asasa's shoulder and said, very quietly: "You found your own ending."

By morning, Agiri-Asasa was gone. The groundnut girl told the story for the rest of her long life.

---

What Remains

In parts of Oyo, there is still an expression used when someone becomes obsessed with legacy and fame at the expense of present joy: "O n gbadura Agiri-Asasa" — "He is praying Agiri-Asasa's prayer."

It is a gentle warning: be careful what you wish for. And more importantly — do not forget to include love in your plans for greatness.

Moral: Immortality without love and purpose is not a gift — it is a slow and lonely punishment. What makes life valuable is not its length, but what fills it.

Moral of the story

Immortality without love and purpose is not a gift — it is a curse.
