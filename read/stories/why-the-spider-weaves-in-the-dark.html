Why the Spider Weaves in the Dark

October 10, 2025 Yoruba Heritage Team

The Day the Spider Boasted

In the days when animals still held assemblies and addressed one another as equals, there lived a spider named Iwin who was famous throughout the forest for one thing: her webs.

They were extraordinary. Perfect spirals of silk that caught the morning dew and made it look like strings of jewels. Birds would stop their singing to stare. Even the wind slowed to admire them before moving on.

And Iwin knew this. She knew it very well.

One morning at the great forest assembly, Iwin climbed onto the highest branch and declared: "I challenge anyone to show me a craft more beautiful than mine. My webs are the finest things in creation. Even Obatala could not do better with his hands."

The forest went very quiet.

Obatala — the Orisha of creation, the white-robed father of human form — heard this from where he sat above the clouds. He did not come down in anger. He simply sent a response through the wind: "Then weave in the daylight, Iwin, so all may see your work and judge."

---

The Unravelling

Iwin was delighted. She waited for the brightest day and began her work in full view of the entire forest. She was confident. She had always woven in the dark by feel and instinct — but surely in the light she would be even better?

But something strange happened. In the bright sunlight, surrounded by watching eyes, Iwin's hands shook. Her threads crossed where they should have spiralled. Her anchoring lines pulled too tight. The more she worked, the more the web tangled. By midday, what hung between the trees was a limp mess of knots, nothing like her usual masterpieces.

The other animals looked away, embarrassed for her. Iwin sat at the centre of her ruined web, trembling.

Then Oshun appeared — not summoned, but drawn by the distress, as she always was. She crouched down to Iwin's level and spoke softly. "Do you know why your hands knew what to do in the dark but forgot in the light?"

Iwin shook her head.

"Because in the dark," Oshun said, "you wove for yourself. For the joy of it. In the light you wove for them — for their admiration. And a craft done for admiration will always feel the eyes and stumble."

---

The Gift and the Condition

Oshun gave Iwin a gift: her hands would remember their skill, but only when no one was watching. Only in the darkness, when the forest slept and there was no applause to reach for, would the webs come out perfectly.

And so it has been ever since. The spider works in darkness, alone and unhurried, and by morning her web hangs in the light — perfect, glistening, more beautiful than anything woven by daylight ambition.

The animals of the forest learned something from this too. They stopped boasting about their gifts in assemblies. They noticed that the most extraordinary things — webs, honeycombs, the chambers of a termite mound — were all made in darkness, in quiet, without an audience.

---

What the Children Are Told

When Yoruba children are restless and want immediate recognition for their work, the elders tell them: Bi alantakun, ṣiṣe ni aṣalẹ — "Like the spider, do your work in the night."

It does not mean hide your talent. It means: do the work first, completely, without craving the audience. The morning will come. The web will be there. And its beauty will speak without you saying a word.

Moral: Patient, quiet work done without an audience produces the most beautiful results. Craft done for admiration will always tremble under the gaze it seeks.

Moral of the story

Patient, quiet work done in darkness often produces the most beautiful results.
