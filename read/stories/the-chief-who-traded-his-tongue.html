The Chief Who Traded His Tongue

October 8, 2025 Yoruba Heritage Team

The Bargain at the Crossroads

There was once a chief named Arolake in a town near the great bend of the Niger. He was not the richest chief, nor the most powerful — and this tormented him. Each market day he watched the senior chiefs arrive in their palanquins, their praise-singers ahead of them, their compounds full of wives and children and cowries.

Arolake wanted all of it.

One evening, restless and bitter, he took the forest path to the crossroads where the three towns met. He had heard that Esu — the divine messenger, the one who stands at every threshold — could sometimes be bargained with. Not bribed. Bargained. There was a difference.

Esu appeared as a small man in a red-and-black cap, sitting on a rock and eating a yam.

"I want what the senior chiefs have," Arolake said without greeting.

Esu looked at him for a long time. "What will you trade?"

Arolake thought. He had no wealth to give. No special skill. He was quiet for a while and then, proudly, he said: "I will trade my ability to speak falsehood. Whatever I say will always be true. Surely that is a fair exchange — truthful speech for prosperity."

Esu smiled in a way that showed too many teeth.

"Done," he said.

---

The Weight of the Gift

At first, Arolake was delighted. His prosperity came quickly. His farms yielded double. Merchants sought him out. He was made a senior chief within three planting seasons. It seemed the bargain was perfect.

But a problem revealed itself slowly, like a crack in a clay pot that only shows after you have filled it with water.

Arolake discovered that he could still speak — but everything he said became true whether he intended it or not. When he told his son in a moment of rage: "You will amount to nothing," the boy withered and never recovered his confidence. When he told a rival in jest: "Your crops will fail," they did. When he whispered to his wife in a dark mood: "This marriage is finished," she packed her things by morning.

He tried to stay silent. But a chief must speak — to settle disputes, to give blessings, to counsel the young. Every word he uttered landed like a stone thrown into a pool. The ripples never ended.

He returned to the crossroads.

---

Esu's Answer

"I want my tongue back," Arolake said. "I want the ability to say things that are not true — to joke, to comfort, to soften. Give me that back."

Esu was eating another yam. "You traded it," he said. "Fairly."

"Then give me at least the wisdom to know when to speak and when to be silent."

Esu considered this. "That," he said, "was never mine to give. That one you must earn yourself."

Arolake returned to his compound a wealthy man and a wiser one. He learned to pause before every sentence. He discovered that silence is often more powerful than speech. He trained himself to ask three questions before opening his mouth: Is it true? Is it kind? Is it necessary?

He never recovered the lightness of careless speech. But his words — when he finally spoke them — carried such weight that the whole town stopped to listen.

---

What the Elders Remember

Yoruba proverb: "Enu ni ojo, ohun ni ojo eni" — The mouth is a rain cloud; what it says becomes the weather of a person's life.

To this day, the elders of that region teach children that the tongue is the most powerful organ in the body — more powerful than the arm that lifts, the leg that runs, or the eye that sees. Because what the tongue says can raise up a person or level a town, and unlike a fallen tree, words cannot be stood back up.

Moral: A mouth that cannot speak truth is not a mouth at all. But a mouth with no wisdom about when to speak is equally dangerous. Both truth and timing are sacred.

Moral of the story

A mouth that cannot speak truth is not a mouth at all — it is just a hole in a face.
